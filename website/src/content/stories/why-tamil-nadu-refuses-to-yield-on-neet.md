While national headlines focus on test security, paper leaks, and central administrative fixes for the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (NEET), Tamil Nadu continues to wage a far more fundamental battle. The state is not asking to reform the exam; it is demanding its total abolition.

For Tamil Nadu, NEET is not a technical dispute over exam logistics. It is an emotional, deeply political crusade for social justice and educational equity. What began as administrative resistance transformed into a state-wide movement following the tragic 2017 suicide of S. Anitha, a 17-year-old Dalit student from Ariyalur. Anitha had scored a brilliant 1,176 out of 1,200 in her Class 12 board exams, which would have comfortably guaranteed her a government medical seat under the state’s traditional mark-based system. Her low score on a single central test shattered that dream, sparking a political firestorm that still dominates state assembly debates.

Across Tamil Nadu’s political spectrum, opposition to NEET remains a rare point of unified consensus among regional parties. The ruling Tamilaga Vettri Kazhagam (TVK) government led by Chief Minister C. Joseph Vijay, alongside primary regional parties like the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK) and All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (AIADMK), have repeatedly backed resolutions urging the Union government to scrap the exam and return admission authority to Class 12 board marks. The Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP), standing virtually alone in opposition in the assembly, maintains that NEET enforces national standards and points to measures like the 7.5% government-school reservation quota as sufficient protection.

At the heart of the resistance is a stark political reality: the exam system disproportionately favors affluent urban candidates who can afford expensive private coaching centers, specialized study packages, and gap years. Meanwhile, rural government school students studying in the Tamil medium face long commutes and household responsibilities, relying solely on standard textbooks.

Highlighting the ground reality, SFI comrade Shamsheer Ahmed stressed the mental strain imposed on youth: “NEET has created immense, unnecessary pressure on our young students, taking a devastating toll on their mental well-being and driving many to extreme distress.”

Activists and educationists like P.B. Prince Gajendra Babu of the State Platform for Common School System similarly argue that commercialized entrance tests deepen economic divisions rather than identifying merit. And many student protesters are saying, " A single entrance examination should not become a barrier that denies equal opportunities to students from diverse socio-economic backgrounds, nor should it jeopardize their lives to pursue a medical career. We demand an immediate end to this system.”

For Tamil Nadu, the issue strikes at the core of state rights, federal autonomy, and the fundamental right to equal education.
