Set in Puducherry, the film follows a diverse theatre troupe gathering to unpack the realities of romance, desire, and honor killings. The ensemble cast brings this vision to life with raw intensity. Dushara Vijayan anchors the screen as Rene, an Ambedkarite with razor-sharp, unapologetic conviction, while Kalidas Jayaram plays Iniyan with a quiet vulnerability, capturing the tension of a man trying to outgrow his own conditioning. Kalaiyarasan rounds it out as Arjun, whose initial casual toxicity acts as an uncomfortable mirror for the audience.

Through late-night debates and volatile rehearsals, these characters strip away their political armor. They expose how deeply caste, patriarchy, and heteronormativity shape who we love and how we desire. Ranjith weaves politics right into the bloodstream of human connection. By setting the story within a theatre troupe, he mirrors society itself, a grand stage that choreographs "acceptable" love while violently punishing anything that steps out of line.

The film acknowledges that unlearning bias is never a clean checklist; it is messy, awkward, and deeply humbling. Even characters with all the right political words stumble over their own blind spots, showing us that prejudice isn't just an abstract monster out there, but something that hides in our daily habits and quiet preferences.

Fueled by Tenma's atmospheric score and grounded by powerful performances, Natchathiram Nagargiradhu refuses to offer a neat, comforting ending. It leaves you sitting with your own contradictions long after the credits roll, whispering a simple truth: real equity demands a radical honesty, starting with ourselves.
