As the Cockroach Janta Party's sit-in protest at Jantar Mantar - Delhi stretches past two weeks, with climate activist Sonam Wangchuk's hunger strike drawing national headlines, a quieter question is being asked inside India's oldest Left student organisation SFI : why did it take a satirical meme account to give the anti-NEET movement this scale of urgency?

The Students' Federation of India (SFI), the CPI(M)'s student wing, has campaigned against the National Eligibility-cum-Entrance Test since its introduction, opposing it as a barrier for rural and underprivileged students. Yet it was CJP, born out of a Supreme Court remark comparing unemployed youth to "cockroaches" that turned this year's paper-leak scandal into a movement drawing crowds and headlines at a speed SFI's own campaigns never matched.

Surya Barathi, an SFI representative from Delhi, doesn't dispute this. "CJP was primarily a response to the comments by CJI, so there's an emotion at play," he said, pointing to the viral anger that followed the remark rather than any sudden shift in how young people see NEET itself.

That reading matches the timeline: CJP's website went live on 16 May under the tagline "Voice of the Lazy & Unemployed," and within days its Instagram following had crossed ten million, overtaking the ruling BJP's own account. The NEET paper leak, which had already forced over 2.2 million students to sit a re-test, gave that online anger a concrete target: Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan's resignation.

SFI leaders, including former JNUSU president Aishe Ghosh, have since shared platforms with CJP. Surya frames this carefully, stating it not as an endorsement of CJP as an organisation, but as solidarity around a shared demand. "We do that for the cause... rather than supporting CJP entirely," he said, noting that SFI has shared platforms with other outfits in similar fashion before, and that CJP's own framing as "a political front of the youth, by the youth, for the youth" undercuts its claim to be apolitical.

On whether CJP's rise signals that organised student politics has lost touch with how young people mobilise, Surya declined to concede the point, calling it premature, adding that CJP's longevity, structure and trajectory remain unclear, he argued, including questions the movement itself has faced over funding and foreign-influence allegations, which he said were better directed at CJP's own representatives.

Where he was more assertive was on substance: SFI's demands, he said, go well beyond Pradhan's resignation, extending to scrapping the NTA and the National Education Policy, in favour of what he called a "democratic, secular, scientific, and progressive education system."

When asked if CJP would exist a year from now, Surya agrees that it has "the potential to continue," but that depends on choices only CJP's own leadership can make.
