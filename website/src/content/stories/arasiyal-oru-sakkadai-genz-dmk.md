When TVK stormed to victory in 2026, it confirmed something Tamil Nadu's political class had been slow to reckon with, that Gen Z had become a constituency of its own, and it was organising outside traditional party structures. Collectives like CJP had already shown how quickly online energy could turn into street-level movements. Into this moment stepped GenZ DMK, a young, informal network now positioning itself as the Dravidian movement's answer to a generation that never lived through Periyar, Anna or Karunanidhi, but is very online.

According to Hemanth, one of the group's five founding spokespersons, the idea didn't emerge from TVK's win; it predates it. It grew out of the campaign against Vijay in Perambur, at a point when morale was low. A casual meet-up in Pondicherry, involving Hemanth, Syed and Vetri, produced a half-joking guess that maybe 50 people might show interest in a Gen Z Dravidian collective. Within four hours, the reach had exploded. The DMK party itself, Hemanth says, wasn't the instigator and it came forward only after the momentum was visible. Tellingly, the group resists formal labels. Hemanth says "We're not a registered party, and we're not part of the party, actually we might not even be a wing." The messaging, Hemanth insists, isn't only anti-TVK but it's an ideological combat. The group won't contest elections itself but plans to support candidates, with further plans still unannounced. When questioned about what they think about CJP's satire-to-movement arc, Hemanth is unambiguous: it's a warning, not a model. He supports protest as instinct but opposes the ideological vacuum behind it that is dissent, he argues, should target institutions, not drift as diffuse anti-establishment energy that could just as easily turn on DMK as on its rivals. He traces this to cinema fandom itself: as a longtime fan and part of Vijay Narpani Mandram, he says this mobilisation "has been happening for years, but now it's out." Ideological cinema sliding into commercial cinema, fans absorbing it uncritically and Hemanth's caution is stark: "Mirror of politics is cinema, but vice versa is dangerous." Where GenZ DMK stumbles, even by Hemanth's own account, is a passing reference to arrests as "diversion politics," left unresolved as either critique of rivals or admission of their own messaging getting derailed.
