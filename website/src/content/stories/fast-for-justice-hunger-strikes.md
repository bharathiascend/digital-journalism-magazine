The Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) set up camp, demanding one thing above all: the resignation of Union Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan. Organisers hold him responsible for a string of failures in India's exam system, the NEET-UG paper leak chief among them, and they're not being quiet about it. Several students, they say, have already lost their lives because of the crisis. Countless others have simply stopped trusting a system that was supposed to decide their futures.

What started as a rally has turned into something bigger. Under CJP founder Abhijeet Dipke, the protest has become one of the longest and most closely watched hunger strikes India has seen in years. Students from the All India Students' Association (AISA), and the Students' Federation of India (SFI) have actively joined and supported the nationwide hunger strikes. Some go without food for over a week now, and the toll is showing. Falling blood sugar. Dropping blood pressure. The kind of numbers that make doctors nervous

“When someone gives up food demanding justice, it isn't just one person's hunger, it's a call to the conscience of a nation. This protest happening at Jantar Mantar is also a wake-up call for the youth of Tamil Nadu, we cannot stay silent until our voice is heard all the way to Delhi,” said Prabhakaran, a member of CJP's Tamil Nadu unit, explaining why the movement has resonated beyond Delhi.

As the protest demanding the resignation of union minister continues Sonam Wangchuk joined in to amplify the request.The climate activist and educationist's decision to fast alongside the protesters did two things at once: it pulled a much bigger spotlight onto the education crisis, and it dragged Ladakh's long-ignored demands back into the national conversation, whether the government wanted that or not.

As the health situation got worse, so did the political pressure. CPI(ML) general secretary Dipankar Bhattacharya came out in support, framing it plainly, this is about the future of India's youth, full stop. Meanwhile,

protesters say the government's answer wasn't dialogue. It was more police. And that's when the standoff really hardened.

Do hunger strikes actually work anymore?

This isn't new territory for India. Gandhi turned fasting into a political weapon decades before independence, using his own body as leverage against colonial rule, and later, against communal violence. That legacy didn't stay in the past. Vinoba Bhave fasted for land reform. Irom Sharmila fasted for sixteen years straight against AFSPA in Manipur, a protest that went global and still, the law outlived her fast.

The last time a hunger strike really shook the country was 2011, when Anna Hazare's fast against corruption forced Parliament to actually sit down and debate the Lokpal bill. It also launched a handful of political careers that are still around today. So yes, a fast can still move the needle, when it catches the public's imagination the right way.

But it's gotten harder to predict. Governments have learned to simply wait these things out. And going viral doesn't guarantee anything changes on paper. Even Wangchuk's own history proves the point his 2019 fast for Ladakh got attention, sympathy, and headlines. Full statehood never came.

So what makes this one different? Scale, mostly. It's not just students this time. It's students, a well-known activist, and opposition leaders, all standing behind the same demand at the same time. Whether that ends with Pradhan actually stepping down, or with the government simply outlasting the cameras, one question keeps coming back around in India: how much does a fast really cost a government that's decided not to look?
