Tamil Nadu has developed, over three very different governments, an almost choreographed response to death in police custody. A person dies after being picked up for something trivial: a shop kept open past curfew, a theft complaint, a routine interrogation. Outrage builds within hours. Courts move faster than governments. And whichever party happens to be in power that week insists, almost identically to its predecessor, that action has already been taken.

The template was set under AIADMK. In June 2020, shopkeepers P Jayaraj and his son J Bennix died in Sathankulam after being detained for a lockdown violation. The case drew national attention not just as a crime but as an indictment of provincial policing, judicial complicity, and medical negligence. The Madras High Court's Madurai Bench took suo motu cognisance within days, ordering the production of autopsy videos and detailed reports, and later initiated contempt proceedings against officers accused of obstructing the inquiry. Then Chief Minister Edappadi Palaniswami ordered a CBI transfer but publicly attributed the deaths to "breathing difficulty," a line the DMK would spend years attacking. It took nearly six years for the case to reach its conclusion: nine policemen sentenced to death in April 2026, a genuinely rare outcome in a country where custodial-death prosecutions rarely succeed.

DMK, which spent a decade in opposition invoking Sathankulam, found itself running the identical playbook once in power. The flashpoint case was Ajith Kumar, a 27-year-old temple guard tortured to death by a Sivaganga "special team" in June 2025. Coverage framed it explicitly as political liability, reinforcing years of law-and-order criticism against the DMK ahead of the 2026 election. Once again, it was the High Court, not the government, that forced accountability, calling the state's initial Rs 7.5 lakh compensation offer inadequate and ordering Rs 25 lakh instead, before the case moved to the CBI. Chief Minister Stalin cited arrests and transfers as proof of seriousness; the AIADMK, now in opposition, dismissed it as "drama" almost word for word what DMK had once said about AIADMK. Watching from the sidelines was actor-politician Vijay, not yet Chief Minister, who demanded Stalin apologise to the families of all 24 people opposition parties alleged had died in custody since 2021, and who became the first chief-ministerial candidate to personally meet 18 of those families, promising legal support.

That promise is now being tested in real time. Vijay's TVK ended DMK's rule in the 2026 election, and within three months of him becoming Chief Minister, four more people died in custody or prison: Venkatesan in June, Sabarivarman in July (his death confirmed on CCTV with 19 documented injuries), P Balaji in Puzhal prison, and Arunachalam in Thoothukudi. The same commentator who once covered Vijay meeting grieving families is now covering his silence: more than two months into office, the Chief Minister has not spoken publicly about custodial violence or police reform. Arrests followed one of the deaths, but the government also appointed an IPS officer previously linked to alleged extrajudicial killings to head the state Police Academy, a signal rights groups read as anything but reformist. Even the optics of transparency have slipped: during the government's first major Assembly session, live telecast was abruptly suspended, prompting the AIADMK to say the "scene remains the same" despite the change in government.

What emerges across all three regimes is not a story of one party being worse than another, but of an institutional pattern that outlasts elections. The judiciary specifically the Madurai Bench and the NH, has been the only consistently active check, repeatedly stepping in where compensation was low or investigations stalled. The media cycle spikes hard and fades fast, resurfacing mainly as ammunition during Assembly clashes or campaigns. And the Assembly itself has never produced what activists have asked for since 1997, when India first signed the UN Convention Against Torture: a dedicated law. Reform commissions get constituted, recommendations get filed, and regardless of which flag flies over the Secretariat, they get shelved. What changes with each government is only who gets to ask the uncomfortable questions, not whether the answers ever arrive.
