Every election season, television studios light up with exit poll projections hours before a single vote is officially counted. They shape conversations, headlines and, some say, markets. But how do ordinary people, especially young, urban and educated viewers, feel about the release of exit polls ahead of the actual results? An online opinion survey of 82 respondents set out to find out.

## Who Took the Survey?

The sample skews young and urban. About 78% of respondents were aged 18 to 28, and roughly 11% were aged 28 to 38, with the remainder older. Students made up 58.5% of respondents, followed by those employed in the private sector (24.4%), while the rest were spread across other occupations. Undergraduates were the largest educational group at 58.5%, and 84.1% lived in urban areas.

## Familiar, but Not Always Following

Awareness of exit polls is reasonably high. Some 57.3% of respondents said they were very familiar with the term. Engagement, however, is more casual. Only about 30% follow exit polls regularly, while 45.1% follow them only sometimes. Television is the dominant channel: 64.6% said it is where they come across exit poll coverage.

When it comes to accuracy, respondents are measured rather than dismissive. A majority of 68.3% described exit polls as somewhat accurate, while only about 10% considered them very accurate.

The open-ended responses help explain this caution. Asked why exit polls sometimes get election outcomes badly wrong, one respondent pointed to the difficulty at the heart of the exercise: predicting an entire electorate from a sample. Turnout, sampling errors, regional differences and last-minute changes in voter choice can all throw a projection off.

## A Trust Problem for Polling Agencies

The sharpest findings relate to neutrality. About 41.5% of respondents do not trust polling agencies to be neutral, and another 40.2% believe agencies may be neutral but with reservations. Together, more than four in five respondents harbour some doubt about the impartiality of those conducting the polls.

The suspicion of bias runs deeper. Just over half (51.2%) felt that agencies could possibly favour certain political parties or leanings, while around 31% were convinced they definitely do.

Yet the written responses show that scepticism does not always mean outright cynicism. Asked whether exit polls are driven by genuine research, business interests or political agendas, one respondent argued that the answer is all three. In their view, exit polls are rooted in genuine research methodology, because without scientific rigour the data would be useless even for commercial purposes. But they are sustained by business interests such as media ratings, public engagement and brand survival. And they are vulnerable to political agendas, "not necessarily because every pollster is corrupt, but because data in politics is currency." When exit polls succeed, the respondent noted, they are praised as triumphs of social science; when they fail, they expose the fragile tension between objective data collection and the chaotic, partisan world it attempts to measure.

## Information or Ratings?

Respondents were also asked about the motivation behind publishing exit polls. Nearly half (45.1%) believe they are released purely for TRP, that is, to attract viewers. Another 41.5% see them as a mix of TRP-chasing and genuine information. The overall sentiment is that ratings play a large role, even if few see exit polls as entirely without informational value.

When exit polls miss the mark, should the media houses that broadcast them be held accountable? One respondent's answer was less about punishment than about transparency. Media houses, they said, should be open about their methods and limitations, and present exit polls as predictions rather than facts. Their conclusion was simple: only the official results should be considered final.

A notable share of respondents connect exit polls to financial markets. About 46.3% believe exit polls definitely affect the Indian stock market. Support for oversight is correspondingly strong: 57.3% said there should be very strict regulation of exit polls.

## Good, Bad or Neither?

Perhaps the most telling result is how divided and uncertain opinion is on the bottom line. Asked whether exit polls ultimately serve the public good or harm it:

- **45.1%** remained neutral
- **26.8%** said they cause more harm than good
- **15.9%** were not sure
- **12.2%** said they are useful

Only about one in eight respondents sees exit polls as clearly beneficial, while more than twice as many see them as harmful.

The survey paints a picture of an audience that is aware of exit polls and consumes them, largely through television, but does not fully trust them. Respondents view the projections as somewhat accurate at best, question the neutrality of the agencies behind them, suspect commercial motives, and lean toward tighter regulation.

The written responses add nuance. Even critical respondents acknowledge the genuine research behind exit polls and the real difficulty of forecasting a vast, varied electorate. What they ask for is honesty: polls presented as fallible predictions, with their methods and limits laid bare. The large neutral and unsure groups suggest that many people have not settled on a view, which points to a need for greater transparency about methodology, sample sizes and funding.

*A note on method: this was an online survey with 82 respondents, mostly young, urban students. Results are indicative of this group's opinions rather than the wider population's. The open-ended comments quoted here are individual views and are not statistically representative.*
