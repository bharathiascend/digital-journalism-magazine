  The 2026 NEET controversy turned into not just an examination imbroglio, but a student movement, which saw Jantar Mantar as the epicentre of dissent, with protests erupting all over Hyderabad, Chennai and Prayagraj. The way different newspapers have chosen to cover this imbroglio is telling. While most Indian newspapers are now run by corporations, the way the NEET protests have been covered tells us how much journalism shapes the lens through which we view the action.

A look at the various reports on TOI show that it has mostly taken an event-focused approach, with visuals being the most important part of the story. TOI seems to focus heavily on scale (how many students turned up to protest), the slogans and soundbites (what the students had to say), police and political reaction (how the authorities responded) and the happenings at the protest spot. One article spoke about the Jantar Mantar protests with words like langar, lassi, strewn slippers and suitcases. Such articles work well on Instagram as an image and a short caption can encapsulate the essence of a Jantar Mantar protest.

The Hindu , on the other hand, tends to be more institution-focused. Its coverage of the NEET row reflects that. In the coverage, it asks more questions of the institution and the machinery, and fewer questions of the students protesting. One may argue that the former is far more important than the latter. However, what makes the NEET protests so noteworthy (and different from your average student protest) is the fact that it is the confluence of an immediate issue (the NEET exam) with the larger malaise (poor governance). While TOI focuses on the former, The Hindu asks questions of the latter. For instance, by focusing on what exactly the students were protesting, The Hindu makes us ask bigger questions. If the students thought that protesting at Jantar Mantar was the best way to deal with a minor irregularity in the examination process, are they aware of how flawed the system is?

The regional newspapers add a different hue to the coverage. When the protests spread to Hyderabad, Chennai and Prayagraj, it was no longer just a protest against NEET or even a student issue. It became a local issue. For instance, in Prayagraj, it was a gathering of thousands of students and young people, holding placards and seeking answers. In Chennai, students and professors rallied in solidarity with those protesting in Delhi, asking for an inquiry into the alleged irregularities. The issue was no longer just NEET, but also about the students' own problems.

The protests gained further mileage with social media. The people making the news became the news makers. Social media allowed the protesters to share their voices, their pain, their anger, their questions. It allowed them to connect with a larger audience. It gave them a platform to share photos and videos, to hold out placards, to take the protest to a larger space. Not only NEET aspirants but also other students and young people rallied, connecting the issues of employment to NEET.

Framing is the most important part of any journalistic piece. TOI covers the event as a spectacle, with the article being a mere caption to the larger photo. The Hindu asks bigger questions. The regional newspapers make the national issue local. Each approach is as valid as the other, but none seem to highlight the larger issue.

In the digital age, where people read news on Instagram far more than in actual newspapers, the headline and the image often matter more than the article. In an era where people consume news in the form of images, videos, stories and soundbites, the responsibility of journalism is to remind us that the spectacle is only one part. There are deeper questions of the examination system, of governance, of student unrest, that matter far more.
