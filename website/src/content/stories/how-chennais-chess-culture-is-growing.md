There was a time when watching a chess game meant sitting across a wooden board, waiting for an opponent to move a piece. Today, a chessboard can fit inside a phone screen. A teenager can play someone from another country before breakfast, watch a Grandmaster analyse a position on YouTube by afternoon and, by evening, walk into a tournament hall to watch the same game unfold in front of them.

The 2026 Quantbox Chennai Grand Masters showed just how much that culture has changed. Held from July 16 to 22, the fourth edition brought together eight elite players, including World Champion Gukesh D, Arjun Erigaisi, Alireza Firouzja, Nodirbek Abdusattorov and Nihal Sarin. Firouzja eventually won the tournament with 4.5 points from seven rounds, while Gukesh finished with two points.

For many players of my generation, chess did not begin in a tournament hall. It began online. Platforms such as Chess.com and Lichess have made the game far more accessible. A board no longer needs to be set up on a table before a game can begin. Opening principles, tactics and endgames are available at the tap of a screen, while players can compete constantly against opponents of different strengths and from different parts of the world.

That accessibility has helped make chess less intimidating for a younger generation. Gen Z has not only consumed chess as a spectator sport; many have started playing it themselves and in India, that interest received another major push when Gukesh became the youngest undisputed World Chess Champion at 18 in 2024. His achievement gave young Indian players something more immediate than the distant image of a Grandmaster. They could see someone close to their own age reach the highest level of the game.

For Revanth, a chess enthusiast attending the event, the experience demonstrated how different following chess online feels from being physically present around a tournament. "I have never been to any international-level organised chess tournament before," he said. "It was very well organised. The people knew what they had to do."

One of the biggest highlights for him was getting inside the playing hall. Visitors were allowed to take photographs during a designated window, from 2:55 p.m. to 3:10 p.m., something he appreciated because photography is often heavily restricted at major chess events.

Once inside, however, the atmosphere changed completely. There were no commentators explaining every move. No analysis bar appeared on a screen to announce who was winning. No online engine calculated whether a move was brilliant or a blunder. There was simply the board.

"You had to be quiet," Revanth recalled. "They will not be analysing bars in front of you to contemplate who is winning or losing. It is all you and your brain." For a chess player, that silence is familiar. It is also something that online chess cannot completely reproduce. On a digital board, a mistake can be instantly exposed by an engine. In a tournament hall, there is only the player, the position and the pressure of having to calculate everything alone.

Yet it was outside the playing hall where Revanth felt the newer side of chess culture most strongly. The fan area brought together commentators, players and spectators, creating a space where people who had spent years following chess through their screens could finally meet the people they watched online.

Revanth particularly mentioned commentator Sahil Tiku, whom he had followed since the lockdown period. Players and personalities he had previously encountered through Instagram and online chess content were suddenly standing in the same room. "That was surreal," he said. "The people whom I have seen play or participate in tournaments during lockdown, I got to see them in person."

The internet may have brought people into the game, but live events are giving those online communities somewhere to gather. There were stalls selling chess equipment and sets, along with food and spaces where fans could interact with players. Revanth even left with a classic chess set from Paramount Chess after being drawn to the different boards on display.

There were also moments that would have been impossible to experience through a screen: standing beside a player, speaking to them and watching the crowd gather for photographs and autographs. For a generation accustomed to watching everything through a screen, the physical experience becomes part of the attraction.

This is what makes the rise of chess in Chennai interesting. The city has always had a deep connection with the game, but its chess culture is no longer confined to traditional clubs, schools and tournament halls. It now exists simultaneously on phones, laptops, social media feeds and physical boards.

The tournament itself may be about elite players calculating variations several moves deep. But around them is a much larger community, the young players learning openings online, fans following Grandmasters on social media, amateur players analysing their own games and spectators experiencing their first major tournament.

Gukesh's journey has made the top of the chess world feel closer. Online platforms have made playing the game easier. Events such as the Chennai Grand Masters are now bringing those two worlds together. For Revanth, attending his first major chess tournament was ultimately about that feeling.

"It was my first chess tournament," he said. "Overall, it was a very, very cool experience for me."

And perhaps that is the clearest sign of where chess is headed in Chennai: the game is no longer just being played on the board. It is being watched, streamed, discussed, shared and carried into a new generation. One move at a time.
