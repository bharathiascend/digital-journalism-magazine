Balakannika remembered the conversation on the cliff after Karthi throws the gun at Subbu. She felt that the scene captured the film's larger point about male ego and the pressure placed on men to behave a certain way. Of course, not everyone thought the film got everything right. Some respondents mentioned problems with the pacing and script, while a few felt certain parts were overdramatic. The ending was where opinions got complicated The climax was the part that divided people the most. 



Still, the majority responded positively. 34 respondents (68%) found the ending moving, bold and unforgettable, while another 13 (26%) were surprised by it but felt that it made sense for the story. Bala was among the few who were not convinced. He felt the kiss scene needed more time because audiences might need a moment to take in what was happening. He also questioned whether viewers had truly accepted Subbu as a woman, even if they understood the point the film was making. That difference between understanding and accepting something came up in other responses too. It was the quieter scenes people remembered When we asked respondents about their favourite or most memorable scenes, many did not pick the biggest moments. Monika, Harini Priya and Soujanya all mentioned the moment when Karthi pauses before leaving and says "varen di". For Harini Priya, that was the moment Karthi finally accepted Subbu as a woman. She said she "cried my eyes out" watching it. 

Karthi's mother was another favourite. Megamithra remembered the scene under the bridge, where Subbu breaks down and the mother feeds her. Shri Lakshmi said she was surprised by how openly the mother accepted Subbu. Nishanthini chose the saree scene, where Subbu becomes Dorothy in front of her family. For her, it was about the courage it takes to be yourself when the people around you may not accept you. Yogi remembered the quiet hanging of Dorothy, played by Keerthy Suresh. He said the scene affected him even more on a rewatch because he already knew the pain Subbu was carrying. For Shri Lakshmi, the final stretch of the film stood out because happy endings for queer characters are still uncommon in Tamil cinema.

But is Kollywood ready? Most respondents don't think it is there yet. 36 people (72%) said Tamil cinema is still largely conservative when it comes to genuine queer stories, especially when compared with films like Malayalam's Kaathal – The Core . 

At the same time, 39 respondents (78%) said filmmakers should continue taking risks and making films that challenge audiences. Star power was also seen as important. 33 people (66%) felt that having a well-known cast or director can encourage audiences to give unfamiliar stories a chance. Bala felt change could happen gradually, with queer characters becoming part of ordinary commercial films instead of always being the centre of a "special" story. Balakannika had an even simpler suggestion: just let queer characters exist. Let them have families, relationships, jobs, problems and happy moments like everyone else, without constantly reminding the audience that they are different. So, what about all the online hate? This was where the answers were less clear. 23 respondents (46%) felt that the backlash online was mostly coming from a loud minority, rather than representing everyone who watched the film. Seventeen (34%) believed the backlash reflected genuine cultural resistance, while the rest were either unsure or felt political trends played a role. Several respondents blamed conditioning. 

"Hate is taught," Madhumitha said. Siva Priya pointed out that audiences have spent years watching stalking, objectification and unhealthy relationships being presented as romance. Yet two people simply loving each other can still become controversial. Yogi offered a more personal perspective. He said he once held the opposing view himself and believes people can understand a film's message without being ready to accept it immediately. Monika felt social media also plays a role. Once Dorothy is reduced to being simply a "gay film", she said, people can reject it without actually engaging with the story. Soujanya had another explanation: social media leaves very little room for middle ground. 

Everything becomes "love it or hate it." What do they want from Tamil cinema next? The answer was surprisingly simple. 39 respondents (78%) said they would recommend Dorothy even to people who usually prefer mainstream commercial Tamil films. And what they want to see next isn't necessarily more films with the exact same story. They want more films that allow queer characters to simply live. Harini Priya wants queer people to "simply exist" on screen without always becoming the joke. Shri Lakshmi wants to see queer characters having happy lives and relationships, not only stories built around suffering. 

Shobana and Nishanthini want people from the LGBTQ+ community to have more involvement in writing and casting, while Ananya Pillai wants to see more LGBTQ+ directors telling their own stories. Maybe that is what stood out most from our 50 responses. People did not agree on everything about Dorothy . They disagreed about the ending, the backlash and even how quickly Tamil cinema should change. But they did agree on one thing: there is room for more stories like this. And perhaps the fact that people are talking about queer identity, masculinity, acceptance and prejudice at all is part of the change. Not everyone is ready for it yet. But some clearly are.
