The 34 year old New York City's mayor, Zohran Mamdani's rise has been difficult to ignore. Emerged as one of the most talked about figures in American politics, attracting young progressives while drawing criticism from conservatives and, increasingly, from parts of the left that helped bring him to power.

But beyond the political headlines is a more complicated question. What do New Yorkers who supported Mamdani actually think of him now?

For Evan, a New Yorker who voted for Mamdani, that popularity is understandable.

"I voted for him. I think he does good work and is super revolutionary in the American political scene, but I'm also critical of him," Evan said. "I think he's doing a great job overall as a mayor." What attracts Evan is not simply Mamdani's policies, but what he represents in American politics.

"He's very well spoken and inspiring and a force to be reckoned with against the oligarchs and far right," Evan said. He believes Mamdani has shown that it is possible to prioritise working-class interests while governing a city as complex as New York.

For supporters like Evan, this makes Mamdani more than a politician who campaigned on progressive ideas. It suggests that some of those ideas can actually be implemented through the government.

But the enthusiasm is not without limits. Evan is particularly critical of Mamdani's approach to Israel and Palestine and his decision to retain NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch. "He's not doing as much as he can for sure," Evan said. "Get rid of the police commissioner, because they brutalize pro-Palestine protests." Content creator Naley, whose political commentary focuses heavily on Palestine and American politics, argues that Mamdani's position represents a contradiction between campaign rhetoric and governing. "I fear that the Democrats have realized that if they run their campaigns on Palestine, they can win elections," Naley said. She described New York's recent Democratic primaries as "the beginning of the Mamdani era." Her criticism centres particularly on Mamdani's earlier promise concerning Benjamin Netanyahu. During his mayoral campaign, Mamdani said he would arrest the Israeli prime minister if he entered New York City. In July, however, Mamdani acknowledged that the city did not have independent legal authority to enforce the International Criminal Court's arrest warrant against Netanyahu. He subsequently called on the federal government to enforce the warrant instead.

For Naley, that change is evidence of a broader retreat."That doesn't take away from all the good things he's done for the city," she said. "But we can admit that he's made many concessions when it comes to Palestine." For Naley, the problem is not that Mamdani has failed to accomplish anything. It is that supporters should not stop demanding more simply because he has accomplished some of what he promised. "We can ask for more," she said. "I voted for him. I can ask for more. I can criticize him." Evan believes Mamdani's political identity is itself being debated among his supporters. Some left-wing critics, he said, question whether Mamdani is truly a democratic socialist or whether he is better described as a social democrat. The distinction matters because social democracy generally seeks to reform capitalism through stronger social safety nets and government intervention, while democratic socialism seeks a deeper transformation of economic ownership and power. "The latter is more extreme," Evan said, "which many argue is needed." This creates an unusual situation for Mamdani. To his opponents, he represents a radical shift in American politics. To some of his own supporters, he may not be radical enough. That tension is particularly visible over Palestine. Mamdani's campaign rhetoric was significantly more confrontational than the position he has been able to implement as mayor. Yet for supporters who voted for Mamdani precisely because of his willingness to challenge established positions on Palestine, the legal explanation does not completely settle the political question. Naley puts it bluntly: "He's given us the bare minimum." His supporters are not a single group. Some see him as a historic break from traditional Democratic politics. Others are watching to see whether his campaign promises can survive the compromises of governing. Some are impressed by his focus on affordability and working-class voters, while others believe he has already compromised too much.For his supporters, he represents the possibility that American politics can be more openly focused on working people. For his critics on the left, he represents a reminder that winning an election is very different from transforming a political system.

As Naley herself put it, "If I don't take the bare minimum from a man I'm dating, why would you accept the bare minimum from your politician?". For Mamdani, the question now is whether New Yorkers will continue to see him as the beginning of a new political era or start asking whether the revolution they voted for has become too comfortable inside City Hall.
